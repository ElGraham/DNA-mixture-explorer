How the residuals method works

  1. Add up all peak heights at the locus and convert each peak to a proportion of the total.
  2. List every pair of genotypes (major, minor) that could explain all the observed alleles, assuming no drop-out.
  3. For a trial mixture proportion Mx, the expected proportion of each allele is Mx x (major's share of that allele) + (1 - Mx) x (minor's share). A heterozygote gives each allele a half share; a homozygote gives it a full share.
  4. The residual is the sum of squared differences between observed and expected proportions. The pair with the smallest residual fits best, and the Mx where it is smallest estimates the mixture proportion.
Why fix Mx? With four peaks the data can pin down both the genotypes and Mx. With two or three peaks several combinations can fit equally well when Mx is free, so use the Mx from your four-peak loci, as the tutorial workflow does.

Limits to keep in mind

  • It assumes exactly two contributors and no drop-out or drop-in. Peaks below the stochastic threshold are flagged.
  • Stutter, degradation and locus-specific amplification effects are not modelled. Remove artefacts before entering heights.
  • Amelogenin is handled with the lecture calculation only, not with genotype combinations.
  • Results are a teaching aid. Casework uses validated probabilistic software (FSR-G-223) and documented reasoning (FSR-G-222).
  • The "clear/ambiguous" call is a classroom heuristic: ambiguous when the second-best combination has a residual less than twice the best.
